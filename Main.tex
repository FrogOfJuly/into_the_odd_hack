\subsection*{Getting Started}

Find a Referee, create a knight, decide if they are a mage, a witch, or neither, and go for it.

\subsection*{Dice Rolls}

To resolve some events, a roll is needed.
Two mechanics modify the roll itself or the dice in the roll.

\paragraph{Upface \& Downface.}

Certain abilities can \texttt{Upface} and \texttt{Downface} a die.
To \texttt{Upface} a die, find it in the sequence:

\colbox{
    \[\texttt{d1, d4, d6, d8, d12}\]
}

And replace it with the next one. To \texttt{Downface} a die, replace it with the previous one.
\texttt{d1} can't be \texttt{Downfaced} and \texttt{d12} can't be \texttt{Upfaced}.

You probably don't have a \texttt{d1} in your possession, but as it always rolls \texttt{1}, you can use any small object to represent it.

When you are \texttt{Upfacing} or \texttt{Downfacing} a die in an \texttt{Attack} roll, do that before declaring \texttt{Focus} usage.

\newcolumn
\paragraph{Repeated advantage \& disadvantage.}

There are several types of rolls: \texttt{attack roll}, \texttt{Virtue Saves}, \texttt{Spellweaving Roll}, and more.
Some of them, including the named three, can be rolled with \texttt{Advantage} or \texttt{Disadvantage}.

To roll with \texttt{Advantage}, add a copy of a die that already exists in the roll.
Perform a roll and then remove a die that shares the number of faces with the added one.
Typically, you want to remove the lowest one, but ultimately it's for you to decide which one to remove.

It is possible to roll with repeated \texttt{Advantage} by adding and then removing corresponding number of dice copies.

To roll with \texttt{Disadvantage} -- do the same, but you must remove the best one (the least one for \texttt{Virtue Checks}, the highest for \texttt{Attack Rolls}) from the roll.

If there is a need to simultaneously roll with \texttt{Advantage} and \texttt{Disadvantage}, remove matching pairs of "-antages" until only one of them remains.

It is possible to add \texttt{(Dis)Advantage} to rolls after the roll is performed.
To do that, choose a die that is present there and roll its copy.
Then proceed as if they were rolled together.

\subsection*{Virtues}

Each knight has three attributes that describe his body, mind, and soul.

\textbf{Vigor.}
Strong limbs, firm hands, powerful lungs.

\textbf{Clarity.}
Keen instinct, lucid mind, shrewd eyes.

\textbf{Spirit.}
Charming tongue, iron will, fierce heart.

Knight's creation starts by rolling \texttt{d6 + d12} four times; record not only the sum, but also individual values of each die.
Each of those rolls determines one of the knight's \texttt{Virtues}; you choose which is which and discard the remaining one. You can use character sheet on page \pageref*{sec:ch_sheet}.

Then, determine the knight's capacity for \texttt{Injury}(\texttt{Injury Slots}), \texttt{Focus} and \texttt{Stress} according to steps below.

\colbox{
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[\ding{170}] Half, rounded up, of \texttt{d12} you assigned to \texttt{Vigor} is the amount of \texttt{Severe Injuries} the knight can survive before dying.
    \item[\textdagger] Half, rounded up, of \texttt{d6} you assigned to \texttt{Vigor} is the amount of \texttt{Lethal Injuries} the knight can survive before dying.
    \item[\ding{84}] The value of \texttt{d6} you assigned to \texttt{Clarity} is maximum \texttt{Focus} the knight has.
    \item[\ding{118}] Half, rounded up, of \texttt{d12} you assigned to \texttt{Spirit} is maximum \texttt{Stress} the knight can endure before suffering a \texttt{Trauma}, minimum is \texttt{2}.
\end{itemize}
}
\paragraph{Guard.} Roll \texttt{d6} to determine the maximum \texttt{Guard(GD)} the knight has.

\paragraph{Choose a Passion. } Something dear to the knight's heart, or something weird that allows them to shrug off a failure.
Indulging a Passion restores \texttt{Spirit} and \texttt{Spellweaving} dice (p.\pageref*{sec:spellweaving}). Consult with your Referee if in doubt.

\newcolumn

\paragraph{Saves.}

To pass a Save, roll a \texttt{d20} equal or below the
relevant \texttt{Virtue}. Failure means negative
consequences, not always a failed action.

\paragraph{Feats. } Knight knows all three \texttt{Feats}: \texttt{Smite}, \texttt{Focus} and \texttt{Deny}. 
They can perform them during combat, details of which are on page \pageref*{sec:combat}.

\subsection*{Injury Slots}

When knight suffers an \texttt{Injury} -- fill an \texttt{Injury slot} following steps described below.

\colbox{
\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Determine if injury is \texttt{Severe} or \texttt{Lethal}
    \item Briefly describe an injury 
    \item Record the damage die of the attack which caused the injury
    \item Roll \texttt{Affliction}(see p.\pageref*{sec:affliction}) and record it
\end{enumerate}
}

Each injury could be:
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Untreated} \texttt{injury} applies \texttt{Affliction}
    \item \textbf{Treated}(see p. \pageref*{sec:downtime}) \texttt{injury} occupies the slot, but does not apply \texttt{Affliction}
    \item \textbf{Healed} \texttt{injury} is removed entirely, but leaves a Quirk(see p.\pageref*{sec:quirks}).
\end{itemize}
If a knight suffers an injury and all corresponding slots are full -- fill one with greater lethality. If they are out of those, the knight is \textbf{Slain}.

\subsection*{Spellcasting}

The Realm is not strange to magic and, though not frequently, knights employ it during their endeavours. 
Some knights are \textbf{Spellweavers}(see p.\pageref*{sec:spellweaving}) and some are \textbf{Witches}(see p.\pageref*{sec:witchcraft}).

\subsection*{Weapons \& Gear}

When picking gear for a knight consult with the Referee, but the rule of thumb is as follows: you have budget of 12 points, these points can be spent on weapons and armour. 

Weapon costs as much as its damage dice. For example, shortsword(2d6 damage) costs 12 points, knife(d6 damage) - 6 points. Each gear providing armour costs 2 per armour point. 
Usually it makes sense to limit the starting equipment to be common rarity with a single uncommon item as a prized possession of your knight.

\subsection*{Focus \& Stress}

There are two important resources each knight has:
the amount of thought they can put into perfecting a task and the amount of effort they can put into a failed task to fix it.
Those are tracked by \texttt{Focus} and \texttt{Stress} respectively.

\paragraph{Focus.} When a roll is performed that can be affected by a character, they can spend Focus to
enhance it. It \textbf{must} be declared \textbf{before} rolling the dice. For each point of \texttt{Focus} spent -- add \texttt{Advantage} to that roll.

One can not only plot how to enhance the roll, but also how to sabotage a roll.
If one character can affect the roll of another destructively by planning in advance, for each \texttt{Focus} spent, the roll accumulates \texttt{Disadvantage}.
\emph{Defense} governs this interaction for the attack rolls, see below.

Usually, a knight has no restriction on how much \texttt{Focus} they can spend to enhance an action, but
determining how much focus is possible to spend to oppose a given action should be discussed with the Referee. 

The order is important, so opposing characters decide how much \texttt{Focus} they spend simultaneously.

\paragraph{Stress.} After a roll is made, a knight can decide that they are not satisfied with the result and try to fix it by acquiring \texttt{Stress}.
For each gained \texttt{Stress}, add \texttt{Advantage} to that roll. Increasing one's \texttt{Stress} \textbf{must} be done \textbf{after} the roll.

Similar to \texttt{Focus}, one can also acquire \texttt{Stress} to oppose an action reactively -- after the roll is made.

Dealing with an explicit incoming danger -- any amount of \texttt{Stress} can be spent.
For split second reaction, like avoiding sudden fall or dodging an arrow -- up to \texttt{1} \texttt{Stress} can be spent.

Opposing characters decide how much \texttt{Stress} they spend simultaneously.

A character can't spend \texttt{Stress} if they are at maximum \texttt{Stress}. After resolving an action, check if your \texttt{Stress} is at maximum.
If it is, set it to zero and the character suffers a \texttt{Trauma}(p.\pageref*{sec:injuries}).

\subsection*{Attacks}

\paragraph{Turns.} A turn is enough time to Move and then
perform an Action, always in that order.

Take the \texttt{Weapon Dice} noted on your weapon(s), shield, and granted by bonuses.
Go through the following steps in order:

\colbox{
\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Declare and spend \texttt{Focus}, if target allocated any \texttt{Focus} for its defense -- they may spent it. Remove matching \texttt{(Dis)Advantages}.
    \item Those attacking the same target roll at the same time. Don't mix the dice: \texttt{Focus} and \texttt{Stress} applies individually.
    \item Remove extra dice added through \texttt{Focus}.
    \item Declare \texttt{Stress} gain. Target declares their \texttt{Stress} gain. Remove matching \texttt{(Dis)Advantages} and roll added dice.
    \item Remove extra dice from \texttt{Stress}.
    \item Now you can mix all dice and declare which one is used as a damage die and which are spent on \texttt{Gambits}.
    \item If the target or their nearby allies can \texttt{Deny}, they may use it.
    \item Resolve \texttt{Gambits} that may result in more damage dice(e.g. \texttt{Dismount}).
    \item If the damage die is discarded -- assign a new one, possibly cancelling a \texttt{Gambit}. Other \texttt{Gambit} allocations can't change. 
    \item Add any extra \texttt{Damage} from attackers who are \texttt{Bolstering} the Attack.
    \item Subtract the target’s total \texttt{Armour score}.
    \item The Attack causes that much \texttt{Damage}.
    \item Resolve non-damaging \texttt{Gambits}.
\end{enumerate}
}

\paragraph{Impaired} attacks roll d4 only and cannot
gain bonus dice or benefit from Feats.

\paragraph{Blast} attacks target everybody in their area,
rolling each separately.

\paragraph{Defense} is declared at the end of the turn. 
Allocate any amount of \texttt{Focus}, when you are attacked next turn -- you can spend any amount allocated to apply \texttt{Disadvantage} to the attack.
In the end of that turn mark all \texttt{Focus} allocated for the defense as spent.

\begin{multicols*}{2}
\label{sec:combat}
\subsection*{Damage}
\label{sec:wounded}

The attack’s \texttt{Damage} is split between the
target’s \texttt{Guard} and their \texttt{Vigor} at the target's discretion; both are reduced by the respective amount.

If there was no overspill into \texttt{Vigor}, then they evade the \texttt{Attack}.

If it overspills into target’s \texttt{Vigor} -- they are \texttt{Wounded}.

Check if \texttt{Damage} to \texttt{Vigor} causes a \texttt{Scar} or an \texttt{Injury}.

If it does cause an Injury -- fill the \texttt{Injury slot} of corresponding or greater severity, record damage die that dealt it and apply \texttt{Affliction} (p.\pageref*{sec:affliction}).
If a knight is injured and has no required injury slot left, they are \textbf{Slain}.


\subsection*{Virtue loss.}

Victims are only \textbf{Slain}, \texttt{Wounded} or \texttt{Injured} if the Virtue was reduced by \texttt{Damage}, never as a result of \emph{Virtue Loss}.

At \texttt{0} \texttt{Vigor} you are \texttt{Exhausted} and cannot Attack if you have moved this turn.

At \texttt{0} \texttt{Clarity} you are Exposed and treated as having \texttt{0} \texttt{Guard}.

At \texttt{0} \texttt{Spirit} your attacks are Impaired, rolling a single d4 only.

\subsection*{Feats}

These special actions known by knights can
be performed as described below. Using Feats
risks the knight becoming \texttt{Fatigued} which
prevents them from using further \texttt{Feats} until
they rest.

\paragraph{Smite.} \textit{Release your righteous fury}
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Use before rolling a melee \texttt{Attack}.
    \item The \texttt{Attack} gains either \texttt{+1d12} or \texttt{Blast}.
    \item Pass \texttt{Vigor Save} or become \texttt{Fatigued}.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Focus.} \textit{Create an opening to exploit}
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Use after rolling an \texttt{Attack}.
    \item Perform a Gambit without using a die.
    \item Pass \texttt{Clarity Save} or become \texttt{Fatigued}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deny.} \textit{Rebuff an attack before it lands}
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Use after an \texttt{Attack} roll against you or an ally within arm’s reach.
    \item Discard one Attack die from the roll.
    \item Pass \texttt{Spirit Save} or become \texttt{Fatigued}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection*{Gambits}

Attackers can discard any number of dice in \texttt{Attack Roll} with value 4 or higher to perform \texttt{Gambits}, each one causing one of the effects below.
The affected foe must be the same as the target of the attack, except for \texttt{Makeshift treatment} \texttt{Gambit}.

For gambits other than \texttt{Bolster}, \texttt{Move} and \texttt{Makeshift treatment}, the target receives a \texttt{Vigor Save} to ignore the effect.

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Bolster} the \texttt{Attack} for +1 total \texttt{Damage}
    \item \textbf{Move} after the Attack, even if you already moved or are unable to move
    \item \textbf{Repel} a foe away from you
    \item \textbf{Stop} a foe from moving next turn
    \item \textbf{Impair} a weapon on their next turn
    \item \textbf{Trap} a shield until your next turn
    \item \textbf{Dismount} a foe(add \texttt{d6} to damage dice)
    \item \textbf{Step back} keeping defense up(restore \texttt{1} GD)
    \item \textbf{Makeshift treatment} of your or an ally's injury within arm’s reach.
    \item \textbf{Other effect} of a similar level of impact
\end{itemize}

If a die of \texttt{8} or higher is used in melee, then a Strong Gambit is performed, and the attacker chooses one of the following:

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{No Save} is granted to the target
    \item \textbf{Greater effect} such as disarming an item, breaking a wooden shield, or removing a helm. \emph{Bolster} and \emph{Step Back} are not affected.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Makeshift treatment} \texttt{Gambit} allows one knight to tend to another one, or one knight to tend to themselves.
Tending knight rolls a \texttt{Clarity Save} and the one untended \texttt{Injury} becomes tended.
If that save failed the targeted knight must survive the pain of misjudged treatment, by passing a \texttt{Vigor Save}.
If this one also fails, the injury is still treated, but increases in lethality, potentially killing the knight.

\paragraph{Death.} Even the boldest Knight can die a sudden or ignoble death. Prepare yourself for this.
When a Knight dies, the player creates a new Knight, and they are added to the Company as quickly as possible.

\subsection*{Injuries}
\label{sec:injuries}

A knight's body is tough and trained since childhood, but it can endure only so much.
Dice that determine a knight's \texttt{Vigor} also control the amount of \texttt{Severe and Lethal Injuries} they can sustain.
To track those injuries, use \texttt{Injury Slots} that record the nature of the \texttt{Injury}, the damage die that caused it, and its \texttt{Affliction}.

A knight gets an \texttt{Injury} according to conditions below. Damage from the \texttt{Attack} causes at most one \texttt{Injury} and one more can come from gaining a \texttt{Scar}.

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item[\ding{170}] If a knight lost \texttt{6, 7, 8, or 9} \texttt{Vigor} through \texttt{Damage} -- take a \texttt{Severe Injury}.
    \item[\textbf{\textdagger}] If a knight lost \texttt{10 or more} \texttt{Vigor} through \texttt{Damage} -- take a \texttt{Lethal Injury}.
    \item[\ding{170}] If a knight lost half or more of their remaining \texttt{Vigor}, but not all of it -- take a \texttt{Severe Injury}.
    \item[\textbf{\textdagger}] If a knight lost all their \texttt{Vigor} or more through \texttt{Damage} -- take a \texttt{Lethal Injury}.
\end{itemize}
Take worse one if two or more apply.

Each untreated injury comes with \texttt{Affliction}; Roll \texttt{d4} and consult the table below.
\label{sec:affliction}
\begin{center}
  \rowcolors{2}{gray!25}{white}
    \begin{tabular}{|c|p{6.4cm}|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Roll d4} & \textbf{Affliction} \\
        \hline
        \texttt{1} & The cut is deep, you are \texttt{Bleeding}:\newline lose \texttt{1} \texttt{Vigor} when your turn starts\\
        \texttt{2} & Pain is unbearable, you are in \texttt{Agony}:\newline lose \texttt{1} \texttt{Clarity} when your turn starts\\
        \texttt{3} & Grievous wound, you are in \texttt{Distress}:\newline lose \texttt{1} \texttt{Spirit} when your turn starts\\
        \texttt{4} & If this injury is Lethal you blackout until treated\\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Trauma} 
When \texttt{Stress} reaches the limit a knight can sustain -- it resets and leaves a \texttt{Trauma} behind.
\texttt{Trauma} fills the spot of a \texttt{Severe Injury} and can potentially cause a heart attack and kill a knight if they are out of \texttt{Injury Slots}. 
It has neither damage die nor \texttt{Afflication}. 
Additionally, if you can heal an \texttt{Injury} by some means, you can't deal with \texttt{Trauma} by those means unless explicitly permitted.

If the knight has no \texttt{Focus} left, they can recover \texttt{1} during a \texttt{breather} by acquiring \texttt{1} \texttt{Stress}.

\subsection*{Short Rest}

It is not enough time to make a fire and cook food, but just right to treat injuries, have a talk, or just zone out to process the latest events.

\paragraph{Focus} is restored with decreasing efficiency.
Each time a knight rests during the day for an hour they restore \texttt{Focus}, but with various efficiency. 
The first hour allows a knight to reset their \texttt{Focus} to \texttt{4}, the second -- to \texttt{2}, and the thrid one -- to \texttt{1}. 
Subsequent short rests do not restore \texttt{Focus}. 
Short rest can't reduce \texttt{Focus} or set it above knight's maximum.

\paragraph{Virtue restoration.} During a \texttt{Short Rest} it is possible to restore Virtue, provided a knight has required \textbf{Remedy}(p. \pageref*{sec:gear}).

\subsection*{Overnight Rest}

There is no or little hospitality, just a safe place in the forest or nearby ruins. You make a tent, fire and a meal.
This is how life is sometimes.

\paragraph{Stress} is reduced by \texttt{1} each time a knight sleeps full night, \textbf{Focus} is restored fully.

\paragraph{Virtues} can be restored by 3 points distributed however a knight see fit.

\subsection*{Long Rest}

A proper rest in a bed, with a roof overhead and a hot meal in relative safety.
No armour is worn, weapons are stored away.
You have time to be with yourself or with those around you and forget, at least of a little bit, about all the burdens waiting for you outside.

This takes a night, a day, and another night to have full effect.

\paragraph{A single Injury} is healed without rolls during a \texttt{Long Rest}.

\paragraph{Virtues \& Focus} are restored fully, acquired \textbf{Stress} is removed.

\subsection*{Healing}

\paragraph{Healing an Injury} removes it completely.
This is a tricky endeavour which usually requires a professional to accomplish and comes with a price tag.

Still, in dire circumstances, a knight can attempt to heal themselves or another knight:

\subsection*{Weaving a spell}

The power of a mage is represented by a \texttt{Spellweaving Pool}: a set of dice that you can use to weave spells. It is described by three components:
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item Amount and kind of dice it can hold
    \item How one restores those dice
    \item What actions, if any, could change it
\end{itemize} 
If several effects grant you a spellweaving pool, those exist independently unless specified. Consuming \textbf{Spur} refills \texttt{3} dice from any combination of pools.

To weave a spell, a weaver rolls any number of those dice from any available pools as an Action from that set and then decides what to cast. This is a \texttt{Spellweaving roll}.
Only one spell can be cast per roll. If the spell does not state otherwise, \texttt{Smite} \texttt{Feat} is not applicable for attacks performed within them.

There are many spells one can learn during their mage training, but regardless of syllabus -- all spellweavers know \textit{Detect Magic} by heart.

\subsection*{Rethreading of the arcane}

When a mage rolls dice to weave a spell, in addition to casting, they can choose half, rounded down, of the remaining dice and discard the rest.
These chosen dice can be stored to cast spells later as if they are included in a roll.
They persist until spent, and count as in \texttt{Spellweaving Pool}. The amount of dice in the pool, both unused and stored, can't exceed its size.

    Your mind overloads with stress, you feel like insanity approaches, but instead of collapse into abyss -- your consciousness splits and now you are not just one, but two.

    You create another knight that from now on inhabits your body. 

    \begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
        \item Roll three \texttt{d12 + d6} and pick two of them and assign to \texttt{Clarity} and \texttt{Spirit}. Determine values of \texttt{Focus} and \texttt{Stress} for your new personality and track these separately from the old ones.
        \item Decide if they are a \texttt{Spellweaver}, a \texttt{Witch} or neither.
        \item Both of you still share \texttt{Vigor}, \texttt{Injury Slots} with their contents and \texttt{Guard}.
        \item Both of you acquire \texttt{Quirks} independently, share corporal illnesses and must switch upon gaining \texttt{Trauma} which are shared between two of you.
        \item You can willingly switch governing personality by acquiring \texttt{1} \texttt{Stress}.
        \item Pick their \texttt{Passion} and, finally, a name. 
    \end{itemize}
Once the splitting event is occurred, you switch to the new personality immediately.

Weapons have properties to reflect their behavior.

\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]

    \item \textbf{Slow} weapons cannot be used if the attacker has moved this turn.
\end{itemize}
Melee hefty weapons can be two-handed: add its lowest weapon die to the attack and \texttt{Downface} it.

\subsection*{IDEA: Alternative injury mechanic} 

Everybody gets five injury slots. Those are guarded by \texttt{d4, d6, d8, d10, d12} respectively. 
Once you receive damage to \texttt{Vigor} you choose an injury slot to risk: roll corresponding die and if its greater or equal to received damage you avoid the injury, otherwise the injury slot is filled.
Record the damage and the die that dealt it on the injury slot, roll and record affliction. 
During overnight rest roll one guarding die of the occupied injury slot, reduce recorded damage by that amount. 
If it reaches zero or below - injury is healed. If you guard is less then a maximum that could be rolled on the guarding die - roll it and increase your max GD by the rolled value.

Also, if just getting injury slots is boring -- one can roll \texttt{d4 + d6 + d8 + d10 + d12} and distribute their injuries as one see fit.
Optimal configuration, I suspect, would be to have as many d4s as possible, but alas. Trauma just occupies an injury slot and is solved by same mechanic that is used now.

If one would consider even more death-spiraly mechanic, then after healing an injury -- die of the healed slot is downfaced.

Dealing with increasing/decreasing injury dice can be a whole area of expertise.

\subsection*{Introduction}

It seems that there is very fun thing to do during combat. 
If your enemy rolls for damage and you spot a \texttt{1} on their die - you can claim it after the roll is resolved.

The same goes for you, but then GM claims it for later use. Let's call them hope \& fear dice for no particular reason. 

The dice sets are both public. Party can use them on their own discretion as a form of advantage.

I would say that you can add them to rolls the same way you can use \texttt{Focus}. Maybe before it. 
That complicates combat, maybe its too much. 

\paragraph{TODO:} There is a problem there that it's not clear what this pool represents outside of combat. 

\paragraph{RESOLUTION:} I like the idea that, after the combat finishes, knights convert these dice to \texttt{Focus}, but GM keeps them. 
Otherwise they can just roll them as an addition to their attack as weapon dice. 
One roll per participating knight. However, that makes overpowered knights even more busted.

\paragraph{TOOD:} There is a problem that this mechanic actually disgorges using weapons with a lot of dice. 
There is no benefit to from rolling a maximum value on a die that would counteract fear accumulation.

\paragraph{RESOLUTION:} I have an idea about weapon mastery that you can obtain after using it for a while. 
When you roll a maximum value on a die of a particular weapon -- mark it. If you have as many marks as the maximum value of the largest die of the weapon -- you get mastery over it. 
For each level of master you can leave one die that came from \texttt{Focus} or \texttt{Stress} instead of removing it after the roll.

\paragraph{RESOLUTION 2.0:} Party has their hope die pool, you can claim a die from there any time to restore one \texttt{Focus} or to roll it as an addition to your attack.
You add a die there if the enemy rolled \texttt{1} or if you rolled die's maximum. 
You can have any die there, but \texttt{d20} can be used only in \texttt{Virtue} checks.

After the fight is over those dice disappear, but before that you can use them to refill \texttt{Focus}. or to mark \texttt{Experience} of knight's weapons.

However, if knight rolls \texttt{1} -- the die goes to fear pool. And that does not go away. 
The Referee can use those in later events to represent vulnerability of the party.

\paragraph{RESOLUTION 3.0:} You can treat fear as stress budget of whoever is adversarial to the party.

There could be mechanics for keeping the initiative, but that might be too powerful to introduce.

Technically, during each party's turn there is a cycling through attack targets that might be interesting to explore.
